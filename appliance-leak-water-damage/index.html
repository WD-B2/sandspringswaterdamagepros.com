An appliance is leaking right now.

An appliance leak is an emergency because the water keeps running until it is shut off. Dispatch runs 24/7, nights and weekends included. While you wait, shut off the valve behind or under the appliance, or turn off the main water valve, then unplug the appliance only if you can reach the cord without standing in water.

Stay out of standing water near outlets. Move rugs and furniture, take photos of the leak and the appliance label, and keep any broken hose or part, because your insurer may want to see it.

The usual suspects.

Washing machine.

A burst supply hose or a clogged drain can send gallons across the floor and through to the room below.

Dishwasher.

A failed door seal, loose supply line or stuck valve leaks under cabinets and into the subfloor.

Refrigerator.

A cracked ice maker line leaks slowly, often unnoticed behind the unit for weeks.

Water softener.

A loose drain or cracked housing floods a utility room.

Water heater.

A failed tank or fitting, which has its own page under water heater leak cleanup.

A hose that fails on an upstairs washer sends water through the ceiling below, which then needs the ceiling repair treatment. If the leak came from a tank, read water heater leak cleanup instead.

Hidden water under the floor.

A slow leak behind a dishwasher or refrigerator can wet the subfloor for weeks. Cabinet bases swell, the kitchen floor starts to cup and a musty smell shows up. This is why a small visible puddle can turn into a larger drying job.

Drying under cabinets means pulling toe kicks, drilling small ports or lifting flooring so air can reach the wet space. The kitchen water damage page covers cabinet and counter decisions in more detail.

Preventing the next appliance leak.

Replace rubber washing machine hoses with braided stainless hoses every few years, turn off the supply valves when you leave for a trip and check behind the refrigerator when you move it. A plumber can add a leak sensor or an automatic shut-off valve.

Cost factors for appliance leaks.

  • Water categorySupply line water is clean at first, while drain water is gray and treated differently..
  • Affected areaRooms below the appliance as well as the room it sits in..
  • Materials involvedCabinets, flooring, drywall and ceilings below each add a remove or dry decision..
  • Drying daysWater under cabinets and in subfloors dries more slowly than surface water..
  • Rebuild scopeReplacing the appliance and repairing finishes are separate from the drying..

Sudden appliance failures are commonly covered by homeowners insurance, and long-term leaks often are not. Read your policy and ask your insurer. Want your real number? Call (918) 306-8113.

What not to do after an appliance leak.

  • Do not touch a plugged-in appliance while you stand in water.
  • Do not run a household vacuum on wet floors.
  • Do not pull an appliance out by force if the floor under it is soaked.
  • Do not throw away the broken hose, valve or part before your insurer has seen it.

According to ANSI/IICRC S500, discharge from a washing machine or dishwasher is generally treated as Category 2 water, so the cleanup differs from a clean supply line break.
