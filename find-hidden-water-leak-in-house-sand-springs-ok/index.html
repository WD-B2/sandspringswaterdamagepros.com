How to Find a Hidden Water Leak in Your Sand Springs, OK Home.

A meter test, a toilet test and a walk-through that find most hidden leaks before they cause damage.

Written by the Sand Springs Water Damage Pros team. Published 2026.

24/7 emergency line.
(918) 306-8113.
Call now.

The leaks that do the most damage are the ones you cannot see. A pipe inside a wall, a line under a slab or a fitting in a crawl space can drip for weeks before a stain appears. The good news is that a few simple tests find most of them.

Start with the cheapest and easiest test, which is the water meter.

Test one: the meter.

  1. Turn off every faucet and appliance that uses water.
  2. Find the water meter and read it, including the small leak indicator if it has one.
  3. Wait an hour without using any water.
  4. Read the meter again.
  5. Any movement means water is going somewhere.

If the meter moves with everything off, you have a leak on the line between the meter and the fixtures, or inside the house. Shut the main and see if the movement stops. If it does, the leak is inside the house.

Test two: the toilets.

Toilets are the most common silent leak. Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and wait fifteen minutes without flushing. If color appears in the bowl, the flapper is leaking. Fixing a flapper is cheap and saves hundreds of gallons a month.

Test three: use your senses.

A warm spot or constant sound might point to the slab leak case. A wet cabinet base points to the kitchen case.

Test four: look at the usual hiding spots.

  • Under sinks and behind the toilet.
  • Behind the washing machine and dishwasher.
  • Around the water heater and its pan.
  • In the attic around the AC air handler.
  • Outside around hose bibs and irrigation boxes.

Use technology when you cannot find it.

Thermal cameras show cool wet areas on walls and ceilings. Acoustic listening devices pick up the sound of a pressurized leak. A plumber or restoration company uses these tools, and a moisture meter confirms where water has spread.

Meters and utility help in Sand Springs.

The city water system serves about 12,000 customers over roughly 150 square miles. Meters and service lines vary by address, and about 4,500 customers live outside the city limits. If you suspect a leak on the street side of your meter, the city water page lists an after-hours emergency line.

Homes on private or different provider lines should call their own utility. Mannford, for example, is replacing 992 meters with advanced metering, which may change how usage appears on your bill.

When to call for help.

If the meter keeps moving after you have ruled out toilets and fixtures, call a plumber. If you already see damaged materials, call a restoration crew too. Hidden water spreads, and the sooner readings are taken the less has to be removed.

See the burst pipe page for what happens after a plumber finds the break. Call (918) 306-8113 for the 24/7 line. The same line serves Tulsa and Kiefer.

Outdoor and irrigation leaks.

Leaks do not have to be inside the walls to cost you money. A broken irrigation line, a leaking hose bib and a cracked service line between the meter and the house can all run unseen. Look for patches of soggy ground, unusually green grass, mud in dry weather and the sound of water near the meter box.

To separate irrigation from house leaks, close the main irrigation valve and run the meter test again. If the meter stops, the sprinkler system is the problem. If it keeps moving, look at the service line and the house plumbing.

Using your water bill as a clue.

Compare this month's bill with the same month last year. A rise that you cannot explain by guests, a new garden or a pool fill points to a leak. Many utilities show daily or hourly use if you sign in to the online account. Constant use through the night, when no one is awake, is nearly always a leak.

What to record before you call.

  • The meter reading before and after the test.
  • Which fixtures you checked and the result.
  • The location of any stains, smells or warm spots.
  • Dates of any past plumbing repairs.
  • Photographs of anything unusual.
