Old or New Water Damage? Signs to Check in Sand Springs, OK.

How to tell a recent leak from a past one, and why the difference matters for repair and insurance.

Written by the Sand Springs Water Damage Pros team. Published 2026.

24/7 emergency line.
(918) 306-8113.
Call now.

A stain on a ceiling or wall can tell a story, but it does not come with a date. Knowing whether it is from last week or last decade matters for two reasons. If it is active, the leak is still causing damage. If it is old, you want to be sure the repair worked.

Here are the clues that help separate a new problem from an old one. None of them is perfect, so use them together.

Color and edges.

New water stains are often light yellow or tan and damp to the touch. Old stains turn darker brown and develop a ring where the water dried at its edge. Multiple rings show that the area has gotten wet more than once.

Dating a stain by its look.
Clue.Often means.
Damp, light yellow or tanRecent or active leak.
Dark brown with a dry ringOlder, possibly repeated.
Multiple ringsWet and dry cycles over time.
Bubbling or peeling paintMoisture behind the surface.
Crumbly, soft drywallLong-term wetting.

Texture and feel.

Press the stain gently with a gloved hand. If it feels cool, soft or damp, water is likely still present. A dry, hard stain may be old. Bulging paint, sagging drywall and warped trim are signs that water has been in the material for a while.

Smell and mold.

A musty odor suggests moisture has been present long enough for microbial growth. Visible mold often means the area has been damp for days or longer. Black spots are not always mold, and health concerns belong to a physician. Testing and remediation follow ANSI/IICRC S520.

Measure instead of guess.

A moisture meter and a thermal camera answer the question better than any look. A dry reading on an old stain suggests the leak stopped. A wet reading means water is still there or coming back. Repeat the readings a few days later, and if they rise, the leak is active.

The structural drying page explains how readings are used, and the ceiling page covers the common stain location.

Why it matters for insurance.

Insurers separate sudden damage from gradual damage. The Oklahoma Insurance Department says standard policies usually exclude some long-term causes, and frozen pipe damage may not be covered when it came from negligence. Dated photos and repair records help show when a problem started.

Older homes and permits in Sand Springs.

Sand Springs has housing from many eras. If your home predates the late 1970s, wall materials may include plaster or paint that needs testing before cutting. Mention the age when you call.

The City of Sand Springs Neighborhood Services office issues permits for work involving load-bearing walls and electrical, plumbing or mechanical systems. Past repair work may have had a permit, so ask the city or the seller.

What to do next.

  1. Photograph the stain and mark its edge with a pencil.
  2. Check it again in three days to see if it grows.
  3. Take moisture readings, or ask a pro to.
  4. Find the source and fix it.
  5. Dry the structure before you paint.

What a thermal camera shows and what it does not.

A thermal camera reads surface temperature. Wet materials are often cooler than dry ones because water evaporating from them takes heat with it. That makes the camera good at spotting suspicious areas, especially on ceilings and walls. It does not measure moisture directly, so a cool spot is a reason to check with a meter, not a final answer.

Old stains can look cool too if a wall sits against an exterior surface or an uninsulated pipe. Use the camera to point to where you should measure, and use the meter to confirm.

Repairs that look finished but are not.

A fresh patch with new paint can hide a leak that is still running. The signs are small. A patch that is a slightly different color, a seam that shows through paint, or a ceiling that has been painted but still feels cold to the touch all suggest the repair is cosmetic. Ask what was done to find and fix the source before the finish went on.

Sorting out several leaks in one spot.

Some stains have more than one history. A ceiling may have been wet by a roof leak years ago, repaired, and then wet again by a plumbing line. Rings at different distances from the center are a clue, and so is paint that has been applied in more than one shade. A restoration crew can map the wet area now and tell you which part is active.

Keep a record of the dates when you notice changes. A simple note with a photo each time the stain looks different builds a timeline that helps a plumber, a roofer or an adjuster understand what happened.

Mold and old water damage.

Old water damage and mold often travel together, but they are not the same thing. A stain can be dry and free of growth, and a hidden damp spot can grow mold with no visible stain. If a room smells musty or someone in the house has symptoms that seem tied to a room, move the conversation to a physician and have the room inspected.

Mark the edge of the stain and watch it. A stain that grows is a leak that is still active.
