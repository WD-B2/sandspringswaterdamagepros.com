Water Damage Insurance Claim Steps in Sand Springs, OK.

From the first photo to the final payment, the order of operations that keeps a claim organized and the damage from growing.

Written by the Sand Springs Water Damage Pros team. Published 2026.

24/7 emergency line.
(918) 306-8113.
Call now.

A water damage claim has two clocks running at the same time. One is the damage, which spreads while water sits. The other is the paperwork, which is easier to build while the evidence is fresh. Doing things in the right order protects both.

These steps follow the guidance of the Oklahoma Insurance Department and common restoration practice. Your own policy has the final word on deadlines and requirements.

Step 1: make it safe.

Stop the water if you can, usually by closing the main valve or the fixture valve. Stay out of rooms where water touches outlets, appliances or the electrical panel. If you smell gas or see sparks, leave and call 911 or the utility from outside.

Step 2: photograph before you move anything.

Take wide photos of every wet room, then close-ups of damaged items, wet walls and the water line on the baseboard. Record video as you walk through and describe what you see. Date stamps help, and so does a photo of the source, such as the broken hose or the stained ceiling.

Step 3: call your agent or insurer.

The Oklahoma Insurance Department advises contacting your agent right away. Ask whether the loss looks like a covered cause, what your deductible is and what the insurer wants from you. Write down the claim number, the date and the name of the person you spoke with.

Step 4: make reasonable temporary repairs.

Policies expect you to prevent further damage. That can mean shutting off water, tarping a roof opening or hiring a crew to extract water and set drying equipment. Keep every receipt, because reasonable emergency costs are often part of the claim.

Do not throw away damaged items until the adjuster has seen them or you have photographed them thoroughly. Keep the broken part, whether it is a hose, a valve or a water heater fitting.

Step 5: start drying right away.

Water that sits does not just stay put. It wicks into drywall, insulation and subfloors, and mold can begin to grow when wet materials are not dried within about 24 to 48 hours, according to EPA guidance. A restoration crew that logs readings each visit is building the record your adjuster will read.

The structural drying page explains how moisture is measured and when equipment is removed.

Step 6: meet the adjuster.

The adjuster inspects the damage and decides how the policy applies. Walk through the loss with the adjuster, share your photos and logs and ask for the scope in writing. If the adjuster's scope differs from the contractor's, ask both to explain the difference.

Step 7: get repair estimates.

Mitigation, which means stopping and drying the water, is separate from repair, which means replacing drywall, flooring and paint. Insurers often pay for them in different ways, so keep the paperwork separate. Ask each contractor what part of the job the number covers.

A note for Sand Springs homeowners.

The City of Sand Springs Neighborhood Services office issues building and trade permits. New construction or alterations involving load-bearing walls and electrical, plumbing or mechanical work generally need one. If your repair includes any of that, ask the city before work starts.

Contractors must also register their state license with the city, so confirm that before you sign. Roofing companies need a state license, and a roof repair needs a city permit only when decking is replaced.

Common mistakes that slow a claim.

  • Waiting days to start drying while debating coverage.
  • Throwing out damaged items before taking photos.
  • Signing a contract before reading the scope and price.
  • Failing to keep the broken part.
  • Painting over stains before the wall is dry.

Keeping a claim diary.

Claims often run for weeks, and memory fades quickly. A simple diary helps. Each time you speak to your insurer, a contractor or a plumber, write the date, the person, what was said and what happens next. Save emails and text messages in one folder. When two parties disagree about what was promised, the diary settles it.

Photograph the progress of drying and repairs as well. Pictures of equipment placement, removals and the final dry readings show that the work was done and that the structure was dry before anything new was installed.

Contents and living expenses.

A claim covers more than the building. Make a list of damaged belongings with the item, the approximate age and the replacement cost. Photographs and receipts help, and so do bank statements for expensive items. If the home cannot be lived in during drying, ask your insurer whether additional living expenses are covered and keep every receipt for lodging and meals.

Photograph first, stop the water, call your agent and start drying. Everything else follows from those four.
