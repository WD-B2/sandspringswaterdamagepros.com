How to Prepare Your Sand Springs, OK Home for Storm Season.

A seasonal checklist for gutters, grading, drains and documents, based on how water actually reaches homes along the Arkansas River.

Written by the Sand Springs Water Damage Pros team. Published 2026.

24/7 emergency line.
(918) 306-8113.
Call now.

Storm season in northeast Oklahoma is not one event. It is a run of heavy rains, wind and sometimes hail that tests every weak point in a house. Most storm water damage happens at places that could have been checked in an afternoon: a clogged gutter, a loose flashing, a sump pump that has never been tested.

This checklist is built around how water reaches homes in the Sand Springs area. Work through it before March, and again before fall.

Why spring matters here.

The National Weather Service Tulsa office reports a March precipitation normal of 3.10 inches for 1991 to 2020. That number is an average, and individual storms can drop much more in a day. Saturated ground then sends runoff toward foundations.

In May 2019 the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers released about 275,000 cubic feet per second from Keystone Dam, the highest release since 1986. It was a reminder that river conditions can change fast.

Outside the house.

Extend downspouts several feet from the foundation, and clear leaves from gutters after the last big drop in fall and again in spring. If trees overhang the roof, trim them before a limb finds a shingle.

Inside the house.

  • Test the sump pump by pouring water into the pit and watching it start and discharge.
  • Check the pump's backup power or battery if it has one.
  • Look at the ceiling and attic after the first big rain for stains and drips.
  • Know where the main water shutoff and the electrical panel are.
  • Move stored boxes off basement floors and onto shelves.

Drains, sewers and backwater valves.

The City of Sand Springs Neighborhood Services office notes that its only local plumbing amendment requires backwater valves on all building drain sewer connections. A backwater valve helps stop sewer water from flowing back into the house during heavy rain. It only works if it is clean and the flap moves freely, so ask a plumber to inspect it.

Do not pour grease down kitchen drains, and keep floor drains clear. If sewage does back up, the sewage backup page explains how to respond.

Creeks, the river and the levee.

The city's floodplain plan identifies Fisher Creek and its tributary Anderson Creek, including the Meadow Valley subdivision. A 2005 buyout removed 28 structures, and new development is regulated to NFIP standards.

A levee of about 20 miles between Sand Springs and Tulsa protects about 10,000 people and is owned by Tulsa County Drainage District 12. Levees reduce risk, and homeowners should still ask about their own flood zone through the FEMA Flood Map Service Center.

Paperwork that helps after a storm.

Photograph each room and the exterior while the house is dry. Store those images and your policy number in the cloud, not on a laptop that could be in a flooded room. Know your deductibles for wind, hail and water, because they can differ.

The Oklahoma Insurance Department says flood needs separate coverage and that standard policies usually exclude interior stormwater damage when the roof and walls are not damaged. Ask your agent about flood and water backup before the season, not after.

If water gets in anyway.

Move valuables, put containers under drips and photograph the damage. Do not climb on a wet roof. A roofer or board-up contractor covers openings, and a restoration crew dries the structure. The storm water damage page explains the order of operations, and homes in Mannford and Sperry face the same problem.

A simple storm-night plan.

Decide ahead of time what the household will do if water appears at night. Who finds the main shutoff? Who calls the insurer and the restoration line? Where do the flashlights and a battery radio live? A plan that takes two minutes to read removes panic and speeds up every step.

Keep a small kit near the shutoff. A flashlight, work gloves, a wrench, a roll of heavy plastic sheeting and a notepad for names and claim numbers cover most first-hour needs. Add a phone charger that works without a wall outlet.

After the storm passes.

Walk the outside of the house in daylight. Look for missing shingles, bent flashing, dents in vents and trees that have shifted. Photograph anything you find, even if no water has come inside yet. Early reports of roof damage are easier to document than leaks that show up weeks later as ceiling stains.

What to do when a storm is overhead.

  • Stay inside and away from windows.
  • Keep clear of wet outlets, appliances and the panel.
  • Do not wade through floodwater, which can hide debris and live wires.
  • Do not drive through flooded roads.
  • Wait until the storm passes to inspect the roof from the ground.

An afternoon of gutter, grade and sump checks costs far less than a night of buckets.
